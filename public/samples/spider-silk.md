# Spider Silk: The Thread That Holds Everything Together

Every spider on Earth makes silk, and almost every part of a spider's life depends on it. Silk becomes webs, safety lines, nursery tents, egg cases, prey wrappings and even parachutes. It is a protein fiber thinner than a human hair, and yet engineers have spent decades trying to copy it.

## Many silks from one spider

A spider does not produce a single kind of silk. Orb-weaving spiders can make up to seven different silks, each spun from its own type of gland and each tuned for a job. Dragline silk forms the frame of the web and the safety line a spider trails behind it wherever it walks. A softer, stretchy silk forms the capture spiral, coated with tiny droplets of glue. Other glands produce silk for wrapping prey, for building egg cases, and for the small attachment discs that anchor threads to leaves and walls.

This toolkit is why spider silk is better understood as a family of materials than as one material. The same animal can produce a stiff structural cable and an elastic, sticky net within a few minutes.

## Stronger than it looks

Dragline silk is the most studied of these fibers. Its tensile strength is in the same range as high-grade steel, while the fiber itself is far lighter. More impressive is its toughness, the amount of energy a thread can absorb before it breaks. Here dragline silk outperforms Kevlar, the synthetic fiber used in protective vests.

The record holder is Darwin's bark spider from Madagascar. Its silk is the toughest biological material that scientists have measured, and the spider uses it to build giant webs across rivers, anchored by bridge lines that can stretch for 25 meters.

## How a liquid becomes a cable

Spider silk is made of proteins called spidroins. Inside the gland they are stored as a concentrated liquid, often called silk dope. As the spider pulls the fiber out through a narrow spinning duct, changes in acidity and the shearing force of the flow reorganize the proteins into a solid thread.

The finished fiber combines two kinds of structure. Tightly packed crystalline regions give silk its strength, while looser, amorphous regions let it stretch. This balance between order and disorder is the secret that materials scientists keep returning to.

## The orb web as engineering

An orb web is built in a precise order. The spider first lays a bridge line, then a frame, then the radii that run from the hub like spokes. A temporary spiral holds everything in place before the spider replaces it with the sticky capture spiral. When an insect hits the web, the radii and the spiral share the impact and turn the energy of the collision into stretch and heat instead of breakage.

Silk is expensive to make, so many orb weavers eat their old web before building a new one. The spider recycles the silk proteins and can spin a fresh web from them within a day.

## Flying on a thread

Silk also lets spiders travel. Young spiders climb to a high point, release a few threads, and drift away on the air, a behavior known as ballooning. Ballooning spiders have been found far out at sea and high in the atmosphere. In 2018, researchers showed that spiders can sense electric fields and that the natural electric field of the atmosphere can help launch them, even when there is no wind.

## Why we cannot farm spiders

Silkworms have been farmed for thousands of years, but spiders are a different story. Most spiders are territorial and many will eat each other, so keeping thousands of them together does not work. One rare exception made headlines in 2012, when a golden cape woven from the silk of more than a million golden orb-weaving spiders collected in Madagascar went on display at the Victoria and Albert Museum in London. It took years of work and is unlikely to be repeated at scale.

## Spider silk without spiders

Instead of farming spiders, scientists produce spidroin proteins in bacteria, yeast and even in the milk of genetically modified goats. Several companies have tried to turn synthetic spider silk into commercial fibers for textiles, medical sutures and lightweight composites. The hardest part is not making the protein but spinning it: reproducing the gentle chemistry and flow of the spider's spinning duct remains a challenge.

## A thread worth following

A single strand of spider silk is lighter than a breath, stronger than it has any right to be, and made at room temperature from water and protein. The next time you walk face first into a web, remember that you have just broken one of the most remarkable materials in nature.
